# Leray overlooking the Seine

Reading references on Jean Leray's work in algebraic topology and the Navier–Stokes equations.

## Requested papers

1. **Haynes Miller (2000), “Leray in Oflag XVIIA: The origins of sheaf theory, sheaf cohomology, and spectral sequences.”** *Gazette des Mathématiciens*, no. 84, supplement *Jean Leray (1906–1998)*, pp. 17–34.  
   [Full paper (PDF, author-hosted at MIT)](https://math.mit.edu/~hrm/papers/ss.pdf) · [Author's bibliography](https://math.mit.edu/~hrm/papers/papers-history.html)  
   Historical account of Leray's development of sheaves, sheaf cohomology and spectral sequences while imprisoned at Oflag XVIIA.

2. **Wojciech S. Ożański and Benjamin C. Pooley (2018), “Leray's fundamental work on the Navier–Stokes equations: a modern review of ‘Sur le mouvement d'un liquide visqueux emplissant l'espace’.”** In *Partial Differential Equations in Fluid Mechanics*, London Mathematical Society Lecture Note Series 452, pp. 113–203.  
   [Full paper (PDF, arXiv)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1708.09787) · [arXiv record](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.09787) · [Publisher DOI](https://doi.org/10.1017/9781108610575.007)  
   A detailed reconstruction of Leray's 1934 Navier–Stokes arguments, including strong and weak solutions, uniqueness questions, and singular times.

## Contact topology and hydrodynamics: Etnyre–Ghrist

**John Etnyre and Robert Ghrist (2000), “[Contact topology and hydrodynamics I: Beltrami fields and the Seifert conjecture](https://etnyre.math.gatech.edu/preprints/papers/hydro.pdf).”** *Nonlinearity* 13(2), 441–458. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1088/0951-7715/13/2/306).

Nonsingular **Beltrami vector fields** (velocity parallel to nonzero curl) correspond, up to rescaling, to **Reeb fields** of contact forms. The correspondence brings periodic-orbit results from contact geometry to steady incompressible **Euler** flows. The “Seifert conjecture” in this title concerns the existence of **periodic orbits**, not Seifert-fibered manifolds.

Companion papers: **[II: Solid tori](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0143385702000408)** (2002) and **[III: Knotted orbits](https://doi.org/10.1090/S0002-9947-00-02651-9)** (2000). These are related reading, **not papers directly cited by OpenAI's Navier–Stokes manuscript**. All three records are in [references.bib](references.bib).

## Related fluid mechanics: shark denticles as airfoil vortex generators

- **August G. Domel, Mehdi Saadat, James C. Weaver, Hossein Haj-Hariri, Katia Bertoldi, and George V. Lauder (2018), “Shark skin-inspired designs that improve aerodynamic performance.”** *Journal of the Royal Society Interface* **15**(139), article 20170828.  
  [Full paper (PDF, author-hosted at Harvard)](https://scholar.harvard.edu/files/bertoldi/files/gus_denticles.pdf) · [Publisher DOI](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsif.2017.0828) · [Harvard explanation](https://seas.harvard.edu/news/using-shark-scales-design-better-drones-planes-and-wind-turbines)

  The researchers 3D-printed mako-shark denticle-inspired ridges onto the suction side of an airfoil and compared 20 configurations in a water-flow tank, alongside simulations. They reported increased lift and reduced drag in selected configurations, including **up to 323% improvement in lift-to-drag ratio** at low angles of attack—not a 323% increase in lift.

  The paper proposes two contributing mechanisms: a wake separation bubble changes the pressure distribution and strengthens suction, while streamwise vortices replenish momentum in the near-wall boundary layer. This is an application of fluid mechanics and flow control relevant to the broader Navier–Stokes reading theme, **not a work by Leray**.

  **Identification note:** Added as a possible match for a remembered professor's social-media post about small structures dramatically improving wing lift. The original post and its attribution have not been verified; the study does provide proposed physical explanations.
