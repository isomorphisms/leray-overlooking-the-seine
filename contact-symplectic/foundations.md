# Foundations: several routes through the subject

These books and papers support different questions: how physical quantities are represented, how symmetry simplifies motion, how ideal fluids acquire geometric structure, and what global contact or symplectic topology can detect.

## Arnold and Khesin: fluid motion as geometry

**Vladimir I. Arnold and Boris A. Khesin, *Topological Methods in Hydrodynamics*.** Second edition, Springer, 2021; first edition 1998. [Publisher and contents](https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-3-030-74278-2).

This is the central fluid reference in the collection. It develops ideal hydrodynamics through diffeomorphism groups, Hamiltonian structures, steady-flow topology, helicity, and vorticity and magnetic fields. Start with chapter 1 on fluid dynamics and geometric structure, chapter 2 on steady flows, and chapter 3 on magnetic and vorticity fields.

An inviscid invariant is a candidate reference quantity for numerical work; viscosity, forcing, regularity, and boundaries determine whether a particular physical problem conserves it. The second edition also supplies an extensive route into later developments.

## Marsden and Ratiu: symmetry and reduced equations

**Jerrold E. Marsden and Tudor S. Ratiu, *Introduction to Mechanics and Symmetry: A Basic Exposition of Classical Mechanical Systems*.** Second edition, Springer, 1999. [Publisher and contents](https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-0-387-21792-5).

Follow Singer into infinite-dimensional systems, Poisson manifolds, momentum maps, and Lie–Poisson/Euler–Poincaré reduction. Useful landmarks are chapter 3, chapters 10–12, and chapter 13, respectively. The guiding question is how equations change after descriptions related by symmetry are identified. Fluid, plasma, and elastic systems motivate the broader framework.

For this folder, the important distinction is between symplectic mechanics and a reduced Poisson description, which can have degeneracy and Casimir invariants.

## Marsden and Weinstein: the reduction construction

**Jerrold Marsden and Alan Weinstein (1974), “Reduction of symplectic manifolds with symmetry.”** *Reports on Mathematical Physics* 5(1), 121–130. [Caltech record](https://authors.library.caltech.edu/records/8k5v6-4pa21) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/0034-4877(74)90021-4).

The construction fixes a momentum value and takes a quotient by the symmetry subgroup that preserves it. Under the paper's regularity and group-action hypotheses, the quotient inherits a symplectic form. Read sections 2–3 for the construction and theorem, then the examples in section 4, including fluid flow.

This is a direct companion to Singer's final reduction example. An arbitrary quotient need not be a smooth symplectic manifold; the regularity and action hypotheses do real work.

## Cannas da Silva: contact and symplectic geometry together

**Ana Cannas da Silva, *Lectures on Symplectic Geometry*.** Lecture Notes in Mathematics 1764, Springer, 2001; corrected printing 2008. [Author's text, revised January 2006](https://people.math.ethz.ch/~acannas/Papers/lsg.pdf) · [Matching website-text errata](https://people.math.ethz.ch/~acannas/Papers/lsg_errata_website.pdf) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-540-45330-7).

Lectures 10–11 introduce contact forms, Reeb fields, and symplectization. Lectures 18–20 develop Hamiltonian mechanics; 21–24 cover group actions, momentum maps, and reduction. Lecture 25 connects momentum maps with gauge theory. Section 11.3 offers a route into the Seifert and Weinstein conjectures.

Cite lecture numbers across versions: the freely available website text has its own date, pagination, and errata.

## Geiges: contact manifolds and knots

**Hansjörg Geiges, *An Introduction to Contact Topology*.** Cambridge Studies in Advanced Mathematics 109, 2008. [Publisher](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/an-introduction-to-contact-topology/F851B2A2E7E78C6B9967A18A6641B40C) · [Contents and original publication information](https://assets.cambridge.org/97805218/65852/frontmatter/9780521865852_frontmatter.pdf) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511611438).

Begin with contact structures and Reeb fields in §1.1, mechanics in §1.4, and geodesic flow in §1.5. Chapters 3–4 develop knots and three-dimensional contact structures; chapters 5–6 lead toward fillings and surgery.

For the circle-bundle strand, §7.2 treats the Boothby–Wang construction. That is a concrete way to connect circle bundles with contact geometry, distinct from the Seifert periodic-orbit conjecture in Etnyre–Ghrist I.

## McDuff and Salamon: the broader topology reference

**Dusa McDuff and Dietmar Salamon, *Introduction to Symplectic Topology*.** Third edition, Oxford University Press, 2017. [Publisher](https://academic.oup.com/book/43512) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780198794899.001.0001).

Use this as a broader reference alongside the focused [Gromov, Taubes, Hutchings, and McDuff–Schlenk readings](symplectic-topology.md). It gives the surrounding symplectic framework in which embeddings, Hamiltonian geometry, and global topology can be studied together.

The book entries in this folder are based on publisher descriptions, contents, and selected introductory material. They do not imply that each volume has been read in full.
